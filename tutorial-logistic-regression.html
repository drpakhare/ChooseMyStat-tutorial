Tutorial 4

Risk Factors for Preeclampsia
Chi-Square Test + Logistic Regression

Walk through ChooseMyStat to identify the right analysis strategy when you have a binary outcome, multiple risk factors, and need to adjust for confounders.

Study Scenario

Dr. Meena, a senior resident in Obstetrics and Gynaecology at a government medical college hospital, is studying risk factors for preeclampsia in a prospective cohort of pregnant women. She wants to identify which maternal factors independently predict the development of preeclampsia after adjusting for potential confounders.

The study follows pregnant women from their first antenatal visit through delivery, recording baseline risk factors and tracking who develops preeclampsia (defined as new-onset hypertension ≥ 140/90 mmHg with proteinuria after 20 weeks of gestation).

  • Outcome: Preeclampsia (Yes / No) — a binary variable
  • Risk factors: BMI, maternal age, parity, family history of preeclampsia, gestational diabetes
  • Design: Prospective cohort with confounder adjustment
  • Baseline event rate: ~8% (based on Indian studies: Abalos et al. 2013, Srinivas et al. 2019)
  • Expected OR for primary exposure (high BMI): 2.0
  • Significance level (α): 0.05
  • Power: 80%

Step 1 in the App: Outcome Type

The first question: "What type of outcome variable are you measuring?"

Our outcome is preeclampsia (Yes / No) — a binary (dichotomous) variable. It falls under the "Categorical" umbrella, specifically a binary categorical outcome with exactly two levels.

Select "Binary" (or "Categorical → Binary", depending on the app version).
Binary vs. Categorical: A binary outcome has exactly two categories (disease present/absent, success/failure, alive/dead). If preeclampsia had been graded as none/mild/severe (three levels), it would be an ordinal outcome, and the app would guide you toward ordinal logistic regression or a chi-square test for trend.

Step 2 in the App: Comparison Structure

Next: "What is the structure of your comparison?"

We are comparing the rate of preeclampsia between two independent groups defined by a risk factor (e.g., BMI ≥ 30 vs. BMI < 30). The groups are independent — each woman is in one group only.

Select "2 Independent Groups".

Step 3 in the App: Adjust for Confounders?

The app asks: "Do you need to adjust for confounders or covariates?"

This is the critical step. Dr. Meena has multiple risk factors (BMI, age, parity, family history, gestational diabetes) and needs to determine which ones are independently associated with preeclampsia after controlling for the others. A simple chi-square test cannot do this — it only tests one factor at a time without adjusting for confounders.

Select "Yes" — we need confounder adjustment.
Why "Yes" matters here: Consider a scenario where older women also tend to have higher BMI. A crude chi-square analysis might show that both age and BMI are associated with preeclampsia, but it cannot disentangle which effect is independent. Logistic regression allows you to estimate the adjusted odds ratio for BMI while holding age (and other variables) constant — answering the question: "Is high BMI a risk factor for preeclampsia even after accounting for maternal age?"

Step 4 in the App: Sample Size Category

The app asks about your expected sample size to determine the appropriate approach.

The Result: Chi-Square + Logistic Regression

The app recommends a two-stage analysis strategy:

Stage 1 — Chi-Square Test: Perform bivariate (unadjusted) analysis of each risk factor against the outcome using chi-square tests. This identifies which variables are individually associated with preeclampsia (typically using p < 0.20 or p < 0.25 as a liberal screening threshold for inclusion in the multivariable model).

Stage 2 — Multivariable Logistic Regression: Enter all significant (or clinically important) variables from Stage 1 into a logistic regression model. This produces adjusted odds ratios (aOR) that quantify each risk factor's independent contribution after controlling for the others.

ChooseMyStat recommends Chi-Square for bivariate analysis, followed by Logistic Regression for confounder adjustment

Why Chi-Square Alone Is Not Enough

A chi-square test tells you whether a single risk factor is associated with the outcome in isolation. But in clinical research, risk factors are often correlated with each other (e.g., older women have higher BMI, gestational diabetes is more common with higher BMI). Without confounder adjustment, you cannot determine which factors are independently predictive.

Logistic regression solves this by modelling all risk factors simultaneously. The adjusted odds ratio for each variable represents its effect on the outcome holding all other variables constant.

7

Sample Size Calculation

Sample size for logistic regression can be calculated using two approaches. Both should be checked, and the larger result should be used.

Approach 1: Hsieh's Formula (1989)

For a single predictor with a binary outcome, Hsieh's formula estimates the required sample size based on the baseline event rate (p0) and the expected odds ratio (OR):

With p0 = 0.08 (8% baseline preeclampsia rate), OR = 2.0 for the primary exposure (high BMI), α = 0.05 (two-sided), power = 80%:

n ≈ 490

Approach 2: Events Per Variable (EPV) Rule

The EPV rule (Peduzzi et al. 1996) states that logistic regression requires a minimum of 10 events per predictor variable to produce stable estimates with acceptable bias.

With 5 predictor variables (BMI, age, parity, family history, gestational diabetes) and a baseline event rate of 8%:

Minimum events needed = 5 × 10 = 50
Total n needed = 50 / 0.08 = 625

Sample Size Result

Taking the larger of the two estimates (EPV rule):

N = 625 (use the larger of Hsieh and EPV estimates)

You need at least 625 pregnant women to have sufficient events (50 preeclampsia cases) for a stable 5-variable logistic regression model. In a high-volume government hospital delivering 200–300 women per month, this is achievable within 3–4 months of recruitment.

Hsieh vs. EPV — which to use? Both approaches address different aspects of sample size adequacy. Hsieh's formula focuses on the power to detect a specific effect size (OR) for the primary exposure. The EPV rule focuses on model stability — ensuring that the logistic regression coefficients are estimated with acceptable precision and bias. In practice, calculate both and use the larger result. If the EPV requirement is much larger, it usually means the event rate is low relative to the number of predictors — a common problem in obstetric research where outcomes like preeclampsia are relatively rare (5–10%).
Interpreting Odds Ratios: An adjusted OR of 2.0 for high BMI means that women with high BMI have twice the odds of developing preeclampsia compared to normal-BMI women, after adjusting for maternal age, parity, family history, and gestational diabetes. Always report ORs with 95% confidence intervals. An OR whose CI includes 1.0 is not statistically significant at the 5% level.

Key Takeaways

  1. Chi-square alone is not enough with confounders: A chi-square test only examines one risk factor at a time. When multiple risk factors are correlated with each other and with the outcome, you need multivariable logistic regression to identify which factors are independently associated with the outcome.
  2. Use both Hsieh and EPV for sample size: Calculate sample size using both approaches and use the larger result. The Hsieh formula targets statistical power for the primary exposure, while the EPV rule (minimum 10 events per predictor) ensures model stability.
  3. Low event rates demand large samples: When the outcome is rare (preeclampsia at 8%), the EPV rule often dominates the sample size calculation. With 5 predictors and an 8% event rate, you need 625 women to ensure 50 events. This is a common challenge in obstetric and rare-disease research.
  4. Report both crude and adjusted ORs: In your thesis results table, show the crude (unadjusted) OR from bivariate analysis alongside the adjusted OR from logistic regression for each risk factor. The difference between the two reveals the degree of confounding. A large change (typically > 10%) indicates meaningful confounding.
  5. Check for collinearity: Before interpreting the logistic regression model, check the Variance Inflation Factor (VIF) for each predictor. VIF > 5–10 suggests problematic collinearity — two predictors are too highly correlated for the model to estimate their independent effects reliably. In this study, BMI and gestational diabetes may be correlated, requiring careful evaluation.
