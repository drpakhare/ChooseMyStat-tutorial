Tutorial 5

Inter-rater Reliability of
Fundoscopy Grading
Intraclass Correlation Coefficient (ICC)

Walk through ChooseMyStat to choose the right reliability measure and calculate the sample size for an inter-rater agreement study of diabetic retinopathy severity scoring.

Study Scenario

Dr. Nair, a senior resident in Ophthalmology, is conducting a reliability study at a tertiary eye centre. Two ophthalmologists independently grade diabetic retinopathy severity using standardised fundoscopy images. Each image receives a continuous severity score from 0 to 100 based on the extent and severity of microaneurysms, haemorrhages, exudates, and neovascularisation.

The goal is to assess inter-rater reliability — how consistently the two ophthalmologists assign scores to the same set of images. Before these severity scores can be used in a clinical trial, the measurement tool must demonstrate adequate reliability.

  • Measurement: Diabetic retinopathy severity score (0–100) — continuous
  • Raters: 2 ophthalmologists (fixed, same raters for all images)
  • Design: Each image graded independently by both raters (blinded)
  • Expected ICC: 0.80 (good to excellent agreement)
  • Null ICC (H0): 0.50 (fair agreement — clinically unacceptable)
  • Significance level (α): 0.05
  • Power: 80%

Agreement vs. Correlation: Do not confuse reliability with correlation. Two raters can have a perfect Pearson correlation (r = 1.0) yet disagree systematically — for example, if Rater B always scores 20 points higher than Rater A. Pearson's r captures the linear relationship but ignores the absolute level of agreement. The ICC captures both — it penalises systematic differences between raters. This is why ICC, not Pearson's r, is the correct measure for inter-rater reliability.

Step 2 in the App: Rater / Test-Retest Reliability

The app asks: "What type of reliability are you assessing?"

We are assessing inter-rater reliability — whether two different raters agree when measuring the same subjects. This is distinct from test-retest reliability (same rater, two time points) or internal consistency (multiple items on a questionnaire).

Select "Rater / Test-Retest Reliability".
ICC works for both inter-rater and test-retest: The ICC framework handles both situations. For inter-rater reliability, the "raters" are two different people measuring the same subjects. For test-retest reliability, the "raters" are the same person (or same instrument) at two different time points. The choice between ICC types (see below) depends on whether the raters are considered fixed or randomly sampled from a population of potential raters.

The Result: Intraclass Correlation Coefficient (ICC)

The app recommends the Intraclass Correlation Coefficient (ICC) as the appropriate reliability measure for continuous data with two or more raters.

ChooseMyStat recommends the Intraclass Correlation Coefficient (ICC)

Understanding ICC Types

The ICC has several variants depending on the study design. The three most common (Shrout & Fleiss 1979 notation) are:

ICC(1,1): Each subject is measured by a different set of randomly selected raters. Rarely used in clinical research (applicable in large screening programmes where each image is read by a random pair of readers).

ICC(2,1): Each subject is measured by the same set of raters, and the raters are considered a random sample from a larger population of potential raters. Use this when you want to generalise beyond the specific raters in your study. This is the most commonly recommended form for clinical reliability studies.

ICC(3,1): Each subject is measured by the same set of raters, and the raters are considered fixed (the only raters of interest). Use this when results apply only to these specific raters. It assesses consistency (relative agreement) rather than absolute agreement.

For this study: Since both ophthalmologists grade all images and Dr. Nair wants to generalise (i.e., the findings should apply to other similarly trained ophthalmologists, not just these two), ICC(2,1) with absolute agreement is the appropriate choice.

Agreement vs. Consistency

Absolute agreement: Requires raters to assign the same scores to the same images. Penalises systematic differences (e.g., Rater B always scoring 10 points higher).

Consistency: Only requires the raters to rank images in the same order. Does not penalise systematic offsets. Use this when the absolute level is less important than the relative ordering.

For clinical grading where the actual score matters (e.g., treatment decisions depend on the severity score), choose absolute agreement.

Sample Size Calculation

Sample size for ICC studies is based on testing whether the ICC exceeds a specified null value. The calculation depends on the expected ICC (ρ1), the null ICC (ρ0), the number of raters (k), and the desired power.

Parameter Value Why?
Expected ICC (ρ1) 0.80 Good to excellent agreement based on similar fundoscopy reliability studies
Null ICC (ρ0) 0.50 Fair agreement — the minimum unacceptable level for clinical use
Number of raters (k) 2 Two ophthalmologists grading each image
Alpha (α) 0.05 One-sided test (we test H0: ICC ≤ 0.50 vs. H1: ICC > 0.50)
Power (1 − β) 80% Conventional power
Dropout / unusable images 10% Some fundoscopy images may be of insufficient quality for grading

Sample Size Result

Using Walter et al. (1998) formula for ICC sample size with ρ0 = 0.50, ρ1 = 0.80, k = 2 raters, α = 0.05 (one-sided), power = 80%:

n = 19 subjects (images) → N = 22 (with 10% unusable images)

You need 19 fundoscopy images graded by both ophthalmologists to test whether the ICC exceeds 0.50 with 80% power. After adding 10% for unusable images, collect 22 images.

Why so few subjects? ICC sample sizes are typically much smaller than those for comparative studies because: (a) each subject contributes multiple measurements (one per rater), and (b) the effect size (the difference between the expected and null ICC) is often large. An expected ICC of 0.80 vs. a null of 0.50 is a substantial difference. If the null were 0.70 instead of 0.50, the required sample size would increase considerably because the effect to be detected would be smaller.
Choosing the null ICC (ρ0): The null ICC represents the threshold below which reliability is considered unacceptable for clinical use. Cicchetti's (1994) benchmarks provide guidance: ICC < 0.40 is poor, 0.40–0.59 is fair, 0.60–0.74 is good, and ≥ 0.75 is excellent. Setting ρ0 = 0.50 means we want to reject the possibility that reliability is merely "fair" and demonstrate that it reaches at least "good" levels.

Key Takeaways

  1. ICC, not Pearson's r, for reliability: Pearson's correlation measures linear association but ignores systematic differences between raters. Two raters with perfect correlation (r = 1.0) may still disagree by a constant offset. The ICC captures both the correlation and the absolute agreement, making it the correct choice for reliability assessment.
  2. Know your ICC type: ICC(1,1) is for different random raters per subject; ICC(2,1) is for the same raters (random factor) rating all subjects — this is the most common choice; ICC(3,1) is for the same raters (fixed factor) — assesses consistency only. For most clinical reliability studies, ICC(2,1) with absolute agreement is appropriate.
  3. Cicchetti's ICC benchmarks: Poor < 0.40, Fair 0.40–0.59, Good 0.60–0.74, Excellent ≥ 0.75. For a clinical measurement to be considered reliable enough for research or clinical use, an ICC of at least 0.75 (excellent) is generally expected. For screening purposes, 0.60–0.74 (good) may be acceptable.
  4. Supplement ICC with a Bland-Altman plot: While the ICC gives a single summary number, a Bland-Altman plot visualises the agreement by plotting the difference between raters against the mean of the two scores for each subject. This reveals patterns that the ICC cannot show: systematic bias (one rater consistently scoring higher), proportional bias (disagreement increasing with higher scores), and outliers.
  5. ICC sample sizes are typically small: Reliability studies often need only 15–30 subjects because each subject contributes multiple measurements (one per rater). This makes reliability studies highly feasible, even with limited resources. However, ensure adequate variability in the subjects — if all fundoscopy images show the same severity, the ICC will be artificially low (restriction of range).
