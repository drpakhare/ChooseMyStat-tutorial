Tutorial 2

BMI and HbA1c Correlation
in Type 2 Diabetes
Pearson / Spearman Correlation

Walk through ChooseMyStat to choose the right test and calculate the sample size for studying the linear association between BMI and HbA1c in newly diagnosed T2DM patients.

Study Scenario

Dr. Verma, a PG resident in General Medicine at a district hospital in Madhya Pradesh, wants to study the linear association between BMI and HbA1c in newly diagnosed Type 2 Diabetes Mellitus (T2DM) patients. The clinical question is straightforward: do patients with higher BMI also tend to have higher glycated haemoglobin levels?

Based on prior Indian studies (Mohan et al. 2007, CURES cohort; Ramachandran et al. 2010), the expected correlation coefficient is moderate positive (r ≈ 0.35). This is a cross-sectional study measuring two continuous variables on each participant — a classic correlation analysis.

  • Variable 1: Body Mass Index (kg/m²) — continuous
  • Variable 2: HbA1c (%) — continuous
  • Design: Cross-sectional, no intervention
  • Expected correlation (r): 0.35 (moderate positive)
  • Significance level (α): 0.05 (two-sided)
  • Power: 80%
1

Open the App and Choose a Pathway

Go to choose-my-stat.vercel.app. You will see the landing page with a brief introduction to what the app does.

Click the "Choose a Statistical Test" button (the purple button on the landing page).

You will see five pathway cards. Since we want to study the association between two continuous variables (not compare groups), we do not choose "Comparing Groups / Before-After". Instead, we need the pathway that handles correlation and association.

Click "Correlation / Association" to start the guided decision tree for association analysis.
Correlation vs. Regression: Correlation measures the strength and direction of a linear relationship between two variables, without implying causation or prediction. If your goal were to predict HbA1c from BMI (or to adjust for confounders), you would choose the "Regression" pathway instead. Here, we simply want to quantify the association.

Step 1 in the App: Outcome Type

The first question the app asks is: "What type of outcome variable are you measuring?"

Both BMI and HbA1c are continuous variables — they are numeric measurements on a ratio scale. BMI is measured in kg/m² and HbA1c in percentage (or mmol/mol in IFCC units).

Select "Continuous".
Select "Continuous" — both BMI and HbA1c are continuous measurements
Why "Continuous"? BMI values like 24.3, 28.7, 31.2 and HbA1c values like 7.2%, 8.5%, 10.1% are numeric measurements that can take any value within a range. If instead you had categorised BMI as "normal / overweight / obese", you would choose a different outcome type, and the app would guide you toward a different test (such as Spearman's rank correlation or a chi-square test for trend).
3

Step 2 in the App: Association (2 Variables)

Next, the app asks about your comparison structure. Since we are studying the association between two continuous variables (not comparing groups), we select the association option.

Select "Association (2 variables)".
Two variables, no groups: In a correlation study, there are no "groups" being compared. Each participant contributes two measurements (BMI and HbA1c), and we ask whether these two measurements co-vary. This is fundamentally different from comparing means between groups (which would call for a t-test or ANOVA).
4

Step 3 in the App: Confounders

The app asks: "Do you need to adjust for confounders or covariates?"

In this initial analysis, Dr. Verma wants to simply quantify the bivariate association between BMI and HbA1c. There is no adjustment for age, sex, duration of diabetes, or other covariates at this stage. A simple correlation coefficient is sufficient.

Select "No".
When to say "Yes": If you wanted to examine the BMI–HbA1c association after controlling for age, sex, or medication use, you would say "Yes". The app would then guide you toward partial correlation or linear regression, which allow you to adjust for confounders. For a thesis, you might first report the unadjusted Pearson r, then follow up with an adjusted analysis.
5

The Result: Pearson / Spearman Correlation

The app recommends Pearson Correlation (if the data are approximately bivariate normal) or Spearman Rank Correlation (if the data are non-normal or contain outliers). Both are valid for quantifying the linear association between two continuous variables.

ChooseMyStat recommends Pearson or Spearman Correlation

Pearson vs. Spearman — Which One?

Pearson's r measures the linear association between two variables and assumes both variables are approximately normally distributed (bivariate normality). It is the default choice for interval/ratio data that meets this assumption.

Spearman's ρ (rho) is a rank-based measure that does not assume normality. Use it when: (a) one or both variables are skewed, (b) there are significant outliers, or (c) the relationship is monotonic but not strictly linear. In Indian clinical samples, HbA1c is often right-skewed in newly diagnosed patients, so Spearman may be more appropriate.

Sample Size Calculation

The sample size formula for testing a correlation coefficient uses Fisher's z transformation. The key formula is:

Formula for Correlation Sample Size

n = [(Zα/2 + Zβ) / C(r)]² + 3

where C(r) = 0.5 × ln[(1 + r) / (1 − r)] is the Fisher z-transform of the expected correlation coefficient r.

Now fill in the parameters from our study scenario:

Parameter Value Why?
Expected r 0.35 Moderate positive correlation from Mohan et al. (CURES) and Ramachandran et al.
C(r) = Fisher z-transform 0.3654 C(0.35) = 0.5 × ln(1.35/0.65) = 0.3654
Zα/2 1.96 For α = 0.05, two-sided
Zβ 0.842 For 80% power
Test type Two-sided We test for any correlation (positive or negative)
Dropout / non-response 10% To account for incomplete data (missing BMI or HbA1c values)

Sample Size Result

Applying the formula:

n = [(1.96 + 0.842) / 0.3654]² + 3 = [2.802 / 0.3654]² + 3 = [7.668]² + 3 = 58.8 + 3 ≈ 62

n = 62 (analytical) → N = 69 (with 10% dropout)

You need 62 participants to detect a correlation of r = 0.35 with 80% power at the 5% significance level. After adding 10% for incomplete data, you should recruit approximately 69 patients.

Why r = 0.35 is clinically meaningful: In clinical epidemiology, correlation coefficients are often lower than in laboratory settings because of biological variability and measurement error. A correlation of 0.35 means BMI explains about 12% of the variance in HbA1c (r² = 0.12). While this may seem modest, it is consistent with the multifactorial nature of glycaemic control — BMI is only one of many determinants of HbA1c (others include beta-cell function, diet, physical activity, genetics, and medication adherence). In the Indian context, Mohan et al. consistently report moderate correlations between adiposity measures and glycaemic markers.
Fisher's z transformation — why is it needed? The sampling distribution of the Pearson correlation coefficient r is not normal (it is bounded between −1 and +1 and becomes increasingly skewed as |r| approaches 1). Fisher's z-transformation converts r to a quantity z = 0.5 × ln[(1+r)/(1−r)] whose sampling distribution is approximately normal with standard error 1/√(n−3). This is why the "+3" appears in the sample size formula, and why the formula works well for sample size calculation.

Key Takeaways

  1. Pearson vs. Spearman — choose based on your data: Use Pearson's r when both variables are approximately normally distributed and the relationship appears linear. Use Spearman's ρ when the data are skewed, contain outliers, or the relationship is monotonic but not linear. Always check normality (Shapiro-Wilk test, Q-Q plots) before deciding.
  2. r = 0.35 is clinically meaningful in epidemiology: Do not dismiss moderate correlations. In multifactorial diseases like T2DM, a single variable rarely explains more than 10–15% of the outcome variance. An r of 0.35 is consistent with findings from large Indian cohort studies (CURES, ICMR-INDIAB).
  3. Fisher's z transformation is essential for sample size: The correlation coefficient r has a bounded, skewed distribution. The z-transformation normalises it, enabling valid sample size calculations and confidence interval construction.
  4. Always report 95% CIs alongside p-values: A p-value tells you whether r is significantly different from zero, but the 95% confidence interval tells you the plausible range of the true correlation. For r = 0.35 with n = 62, the 95% CI is approximately (0.11, 0.55) — confirming a positive but imprecisely estimated association.
  5. Correlation is not causation: A positive BMI–HbA1c correlation does not prove that higher BMI causes higher HbA1c. Confounders such as physical inactivity, dietary patterns, and genetic predisposition may explain part of the observed association. For causal inference, consider multivariable regression or a longitudinal design.
