Tutorial 1

Effect of Yoga on Blood Pressure
Paired t-test

Walk through ChooseMyStat step by step to choose the right test and calculate the sample size for a pre-post yoga intervention study.

Study Scenario

Dr. Sharma, a PG resident in Community Medicine at a tertiary hospital (AIIMS-like setting), wants to study whether a 12-week structured yoga programme can reduce systolic blood pressure (SBP) in adults with stage 1 hypertension.

The design is straightforward: measure each patient's SBP before starting yoga, then measure it again after 12 weeks of the intervention. The same subjects are measured twice — a classic pre-post or before-after study.

  • Outcome: Systolic blood pressure (mmHg) — a continuous variable
  • Design: Paired / before-after (each subject is their own control)
  • Expected mean reduction (δ): 8 mmHg (based on Cramer et al. 2014 meta-analysis)
  • SD of paired differences: 15 mmHg (from Hagins et al. 2013, Indian yoga studies)
  • Significance level (α): 0.05 (two-sided)
  • Power: 80%

Step 1 in the App: Outcome Type

The first question the app asks is: "What type of outcome variable are you measuring?"

Our outcome is systolic blood pressure measured in millimetres of mercury (mmHg). This is a continuous variable — it can take any numeric value within a range and is measured on an interval/ratio scale.

Why "Continuous"? Blood pressure readings are numeric measurements on a continuous scale (e.g., 132 mmHg, 141.5 mmHg). If instead our outcome were categories like "hypertensive / normotensive", we would choose "Categorical". If it were an ordinal score (e.g., pain on a 0–10 scale with few levels), we might choose "Ordinal".
Select "Continuous".
Select "Continuous" for blood pressure in mmHg
3

Step 2 in the App: Comparison Structure

Next, the app asks: "What is the structure of your comparison?"

In our study, the same subjects are measured twice — once before the yoga programme and once after. This is a paired (or before-after) design with two time points.

Paired vs. Independent: The key distinction is whether the two sets of measurements come from the same individuals or from different groups. Here, each patient serves as their own control, so the measurements are paired. If we were comparing a yoga group against a separate control group, the comparison would be "2 Independent Groups" instead.
Select "2 Paired / Before-After".
Select "2 Paired / Before-After" for the pre-post design
4

Step 3 in the App: Distribution

The app now asks: "Are the differences (or the data) approximately normally distributed?"

For the paired t-test, what matters is whether the within-subject differences (post-SBP minus pre-SBP for each patient) follow a roughly normal distribution. Blood pressure differences are generally normally distributed in clinical samples of moderate size (n ≥ 20–30). In our planned study, we expect around 30 subjects, so this assumption is reasonable.

When to say "No": If your sample is very small (say, n < 15) and you have reason to believe the differences are heavily skewed — for example, if you are measuring highly skewed outcomes like hospital costs or antibody titres — you should choose "No". The app will then recommend the Wilcoxon signed-rank test, the non-parametric counterpart of the paired t-test.
Select "Yes, Normal".
Select "Yes, Normal" — BP differences are typically normal in moderate samples
5

Step 4 in the App: Confounders

The final question: "Do you need to adjust for confounders or covariates?"

In this simple pre-post study, we are making a straightforward comparison: did SBP change after yoga? We are not adjusting for age, BMI, baseline medication use, or any other variables in this primary analysis.

When to say "Yes": If your study had additional variables you wanted to control for — for instance, if you wanted to adjust for baseline BMI or concurrent medication use — you would say "Yes". The app would then guide you toward a more complex model such as linear mixed models or repeated-measures ANCOVA.
Select "No".
Select "No" for a simple paired comparison without covariate adjustment

The Result: Paired t-test Recommended

The app now shows you the recommended statistical test: Paired t-test. This is exactly the right test for comparing two continuous measurements taken on the same subjects when the differences are approximately normally distributed.

Sample Size Result

The app calculates:

n = 28 (analytical) → N = 33 (with 15% dropout)

You need 28 subjects to detect an 8 mmHg reduction in SBP with 80% power at the 5% significance level. After adding 15% to account for dropout, you should recruit 33 patients.

Result: n = 28 (analytical), N = 33 (with 15% dropout adjustment)
Where do δ and SD come from? These are the two most critical inputs for any sample size calculation. You should derive them from published literature (pilot studies, meta-analyses, or similar studies in your population) or from your own pilot data. Never guess these values — a poorly chosen δ or SD will give you a study that is either underpowered (misses a real effect) or wasteful (recruits far more patients than needed). For this tutorial, we used the Cramer et al. 2014 meta-analysis and Hagins et al. 2013 as references.

Key Takeaways

  1. Why the paired t-test fits this scenario: We have a continuous outcome (SBP), two measurements on the same subjects (before and after), approximately normal differences, and no need to adjust for confounders. The paired t-test is the natural choice.
  2. When to use the Wilcoxon signed-rank test instead: If your sample is very small or the within-subject differences are clearly non-normal (heavily skewed or with outliers), choose "No" at the distribution step. The app will recommend the Wilcoxon signed-rank test, which does not assume normality.
  3. Always specify δ and SD from literature: The two most important inputs for sample size are the expected mean difference (δ) and the standard deviation of paired differences (SD). Look these up in published meta-analyses, systematic reviews, or pilot studies from your population. Do not invent these numbers.
  4. Account for dropout: In any longitudinal study, some participants will be lost to follow-up. Adding 10–20% to your calculated sample size (depending on the study duration and setting) ensures you retain adequate power even after dropouts.
  5. Limitations of the pre-post design: A simple before-after study without a control group cannot rule out confounders such as regression to the mean, seasonal variation, or concurrent medications. For stronger causal inference, consider a randomised controlled trial with a parallel control group (which would use the independent-samples t-test or ANCOVA).
